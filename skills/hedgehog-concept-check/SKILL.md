# Hedgehog Concept check

Jim Collins's Good to Great (2001) compares companies that made a lasting jump in results with similar companies that didn't. One pattern he describes is the Hedgehog Concept: a simple, clear idea that sits where three circles overlap. The circles are what the people are deeply passionate about, what the company can be the best in the world at, and what drives its economic engine, measured by one ratio, the economic denominator. Collins says the companies that made the jump didn't reach it as a goal. They kept making decisions that fitted it, over years. This skill checks a company, team or product line against the three circles and turns the gaps into decisions.

## When to use

- The team does many things moderately well and can't say what it should be best at.
- Before adding a product line, a market or a service, to test whether it fits.
- During annual planning, to write a stop-doing list.
- Not for analyzing an industry's profitability: use five-forces-analysis. Not for testing whether an advantage is durable against rivals: use vrio-analysis.

## What you need

- What the company or team does today: products, services, segments, with revenue or effort for each if known.
- Results by line: margin, growth, retention, or the measure the team trusts.
- Where the company has beaten rivals, and where it has lost, with evidence.
- What the people say they care about, and what they choose to work on when they can choose.
- If results by line are missing, ask once, then mark claims "[assumption]". Never invent margins or rankings.

## Steps

1. **List the activities.** Write each product line, segment or service as one row, with its share of revenue and of effort.
2. **Fill the best-in-the-world circle.** For each row, say whether the company could be the best at it in a defined field (a niche, a region, a customer type), with evidence: wins against named rivals, ratings, retention, referrals. Collins stresses that this is about what you can be best at, not what you want to be best at, and that a core competence is not enough if others do it better. Mark rows where the company is merely competent.
3. **Fill the economic engine circle.** Propose one or two candidate economic denominators: the single "profit per X" (or "cash per X") that, if raised, would most improve results. Examples: profit per customer visit, gross margin per project, profit per region. Test each against the numbers given and pick one, saying why.
4. **Fill the passion circle.** From what people choose to work on, what they talk about, and what the input says, note which rows the people care about. Passion is not a slogan; it is what people keep choosing to do. Mark it "[check]" when the input doesn't show it.
5. **Write the overlap.** Write one plain sentence for what sits in all three circles. If no row sits in all three, say so: that is the finding, and the next step is research, not a slogan.
6. **Draft the stop-doing list.** List the rows outside the overlap, with what stopping, shrinking, selling or handing them to a partner would free up. Order by effort freed.
7. **Check the people.** Collins's "first who, then what": list the seats the overlap needs and whether the right person is in each. Flag seats that are empty or held by someone who doesn't fit, as hypotheses for the leader.
8. **Sketch the flywheel.** Write the 4 to 6 steps by which the overlap builds momentum (for example: better reviews, more referrals, lower acquisition cost, more spend on quality), and the first step to push this quarter.

## Output format

```markdown
# Hedgehog Concept check: [company or team]

**Date:** [date] · **Owner:** [name]

## Activities in the three circles
| Activity | Share of revenue / effort | Best in the world at? (field, evidence) | Drives the engine? | Passion? | In all three? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Economic denominator
**Chosen:** [profit or cash per X] — [why, with numbers]
**Rejected:** [candidate] — [why]

## The overlap
[One sentence, or "No activity sits in all three circles yet" and what to find out.]

## Stop doing
| Activity | Stop / shrink / sell / partner | What it frees |
|---|---|---|

## Right people for the overlap
| Seat | Who holds it | Fit (hypothesis) |
|---|---|---|

## Flywheel
1. [step] → 2. [step] → 3. [step] → 4. [step] → back to 1
**First push this quarter:** [action, owner, date]
```

## Example

**Input:** "We're a 22-person bike shop group with 3 stores in Lyon. Revenue last year: new bikes 58%, repairs and servicing 27%, rental for tourists 15%. Gross margin: new bikes 24%, servicing 61%, rental 38%. We win servicing customers from the big chains: our Google rating for servicing is 4.8 against their 3.9, and 40% of new bike buyers first came to us for a repair. Rentals are seasonal and two staff hate running them. Our mechanics run a free Saturday repair class that's full every week, nobody asked them to. On new bikes, Decathlon beats us on price."

**Output:**

```markdown
# Hedgehog Concept check: Lyon bike shop group

**Date:** [date] · **Owner:** [check: owner]

## Activities in the three circles
| Activity | Share of revenue / effort | Best in the world at? (field, evidence) | Drives the engine? | Passion? | In all three? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Servicing and repairs | 27% / [check: effort] | Yes, in Lyon: rating 4.8 vs the chains' 3.9 | Yes: 61% margin, and 40% of bike buyers start here | Yes: the mechanics run a full free class unasked | Yes |
| New bikes | 58% / [check] | No: Decathlon wins on price | Partly: 24% margin; sales follow servicing | [check] | No |
| Tourist rental | 15% / [check] | No evidence | 38% margin, seasonal | No: two staff dislike it | No |

## Economic denominator
**Chosen:** gross profit per serviced customer per year — servicing has the best margin and leads to 40% of bike sales, so raising it lifts both lines [check: repeat-visit data].
**Rejected:** profit per store — it hides which line earns the money.

## The overlap
Being the most trusted bike mechanics in Lyon.

## Stop doing
| Activity | Stop / shrink / sell / partner | What it frees |
|---|---|---|
| Tourist rental | Partner with a rental firm or sell the fleet [check: fleet value] | Two staff and summer floor space |
| Bikes stocked to compete on price | Shrink to models that servicing customers ask for | Stock cash [check: amount] |

## Right people for the overlap
| Seat | Who holds it | Fit (hypothesis) |
|---|---|---|
| Head of servicing across 3 stores | [check] | [check] |
| Class lead (the Saturday class) | The mechanics who started it | Strong fit |

## Flywheel
1. Better servicing → 2. higher ratings and class sign-ups → 3. more first visits → 4. more bike sales to people who trust us → 5. more spend on mechanics and tools → back to 1
**First push this quarter:** track gross profit per serviced customer per store [check: owner, date].
```

## Checks before you send

- [ ] Best-in-the-world claims name a field and cite evidence from the input, or are marked "[check]".
- [ ] One economic denominator is chosen, with the reason and the rejected candidate.
- [ ] The overlap is one plain sentence, or the output says plainly that nothing sits in all three.
- [ ] The stop-doing list names real activities from the input, with what each frees.
- [ ] People findings are labelled as hypotheses, never as judgments of a named person.
- [ ] No margin, share or ranking appears that isn't in the input.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an aspiration ("be the best") instead of what the evidence says you can be best at.
- Choosing several denominators. One ratio forces the choice.
- Keeping every activity "because it makes money". Profitable work outside the overlap still takes attention.
- Treating the concept as a slogan to announce. Collins describes it as the result of long, repeated discussion and evidence.
- Taking the book's companies as proof. Critics such as Phil Rosenzweig point to halo effects in the research; treat the circles as a thinking tool.
