# Care and challenge feedback check

Kim Scott, who worked at Google and Apple, wrote Radical Candor (2017; a revised edition followed). It places feedback on two dimensions: how much you care personally about the person, and how much you challenge them directly. Doing both at once is what she calls radical candor. Caring without challenging is ruinous empathy: staying quiet or sugar-coating. Challenging without showing care is obnoxious aggression. Doing neither is manipulative insincerity: praise to someone's face, criticism behind their back. Her site stresses that these are behaviours everyone slips into, not personality types, and asks people not to write anyone's name in the boxes. Scott also advises managers to ask for criticism before giving it. This skill places a planned piece of feedback, rewrites it, and plans the ask.

## When to use

- Before giving praise or criticism that matters, to check it is both kind and clear.
- A manager keeps putting off feedback, or their feedback lands as harsh.
- A team wants a habit of asking for and giving feedback.
- Not for the structure of a single feedback message: use feedback-sbi, which this skill can hand the rewrite to. Not for a formal review: use performance-review-draft.

## What you need

- The feedback as the manager would say it now, or a description of how they usually give it.
- The situation and the specific behaviour, with its effect.
- The relationship: how long they've worked together, what the manager knows the person cares about.
- If the behaviour is vague, ask for one specific example. Never invent events.

## Steps

1. **Separate behaviour from person.** Rewrite the feedback so it is about something the person did, not who they are. Flag labels like "careless" or "not a team player".
2. **Rate the care.** Does the message show the manager cares about the person's success? Look for a reason the feedback helps them, a sign the manager knows them, and a private setting for criticism. Rate low or high.
3. **Rate the challenge.** Is the message clear enough that the person would know exactly what to change? Rate low or high.
4. **Place it.** Name the quadrant from the two ratings and quote the words that put it there. Describe the behaviour, never the manager, with a type.
5. **Rewrite it to do both.** Keep it short, specific, and given soon after the event. Scott advises giving praise in public and criticism in private, and being as specific about praise as about criticism.
6. **Plan the ask for criticism.** Draft one question the manager asks the person first or soon ("What could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?"), and how to respond to what they hear without defending.
7. **Close the loop.** Name when the manager will check in, and what change would show it worked.

## Output format

```markdown
# Feedback check: [situation]

## The feedback as planned
"[text]"

## Behaviour, not person
[flags and rewrites]

## Ratings
**Care personally:** low / high — [evidence] · **Challenge directly:** low / high — [evidence]
**Quadrant:** [ruinous empathy / obnoxious aggression / manipulative insincerity / both at once] — because "[words]"

## Rewrite
**Where and when:** [private or public, when]
"[rewritten feedback]"

## Ask for criticism
**Question:** "[question]" · **If you hear criticism:** [how to respond]

## Follow-up
**Check in:** [when] · **Signal it worked:** [change]
```

## Example

**Input:** "My designer Ana's mockups for the checkout redesign came in two days late for the second sprint running, and the developers had to wait. I was going to say in our team stand-up: 'Great work as always Ana, maybe try to be a bit quicker next time if you can?' I know she's been taking on extra requests from the sales team."

**Output:**

```markdown
# Feedback check: checkout mockups late two sprints running

## The feedback as planned
"Great work as always Ana, maybe try to be a bit quicker next time if you can?"

## Behaviour, not person
The behaviour is clear in your notes (mockups two days late, two sprints running, developers waiting), but the planned words don't say it.

## Ratings
**Care personally:** low to medium — friendly tone, but in a group stand-up and with no sign you know about the sales requests. · **Challenge directly:** low — "a bit quicker… if you can" doesn't say what was late or why it matters.
**Quadrant:** ruinous empathy — because "maybe try to be a bit quicker next time if you can?" sugar-coats the problem, and "Great work as always" blurs it.

## Rewrite
**Where and when:** in private, today or tomorrow.
"Ana, the checkout mockups came in two days after the sprint deadline for the second sprint in a row, and the developers waited both times. I know sales has been sending you extra requests. I want you to be able to do your best work without that squeeze. Can we look at what's on your plate and agree what moves, so the next mockups land on the deadline?"

## Ask for criticism
**Question:** "Is there something I'm doing, like how I set deadlines or handle sales requests, that makes this harder?" · **If you hear criticism:** thank her, ask for an example, and say what you'll change.

## Follow-up
**Check in:** at the start of next sprint · **Signal it worked:** mockups on the deadline, and sales requests routed through you.
```

## Checks before you send

- [ ] The feedback names a specific behaviour and its effect, not a trait.
- [ ] Both ratings cite words from the input.
- [ ] The quadrant describes the feedback, never labels the manager or the person.
- [ ] Criticism is planned in private; praise may be public.
- [ ] No events, motives or history are invented.

## Common mistakes

- Using the quadrant names to label people. Scott's site asks not to.
- Adding a compliment to soften criticism. The criticism gets lost.
- Being "candid" without care. Clear words with no interest in the person land as aggression.
- Only giving, never asking. The ask for criticism is what makes the habit two-way.
