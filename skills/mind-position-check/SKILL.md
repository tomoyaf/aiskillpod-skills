# Mind position check

Al Ries and Jack Trout wrote about positioning in Industrial Marketing in 1969 and in Advertising Age in 1972, and published Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind in 1981. Their starting point: buyers are flooded with messages and keep only what fits a slot already in their mind. Positioning is not what you do to a product; it is what you do to the prospect's mind. For each category, buyers keep a short ladder of brands. The easiest way into the mind is to be first in a category. If you can't be first, relate yourself to the leader, or create a new category where you are first. This skill maps the ladder for a category, finds an open position, and names the one word to own.

## When to use

- A brand says many things and buyers remember none of them.
- A challenger needs a way to compete with a dominant leader.
- Before extending a brand name to a new product line, or naming a new product.
- Not for the full set of positioning components (alternatives, attributes, value, segment, category): use positioning-statement, based on April Dunford's method. Not for choosing the first market segment: use beachhead-plan.

## What you need

- The category as buyers name it, and the brand or product to position.
- The rivals buyers mention, ideally in buyers' own words (reviews, interviews, search terms).
- What the brand actually does better, with evidence.
- Product lines sold under the same name.
- If buyers' words are missing, mark the ladder "[check: buyer research]". Never invent market shares or survey results.

## Steps

1. **Name the category as buyers do.** Use the words buyers use when they search or ask a friend, not the industry's term.
2. **Draw the ladder.** List the brands buyers recall for that category, in order, with the word or idea each one owns. Mark the evidence for each rung.
3. **Find where the brand stands.** Is it on the ladder, and on which rung? What word, if any, do buyers already link to it?
4. **Pick a strategy.**
   - First in the mind: if the brand leads, reinforce the category and the original word; don't chase every new idea.
   - Against the leader: if the brand is second or lower, define itself in relation to the leader (the leader's strength creates its opposite weakness).
   - New category: if the ladder is full, find a narrower or different category where the brand can be first.
   - Repositioning a rival: only with true facts that change how buyers see the rival.
5. **Choose one word.** Pick the single word or short phrase the brand will own. It must be true, distinct from what the leader owns, and something buyers care about.
6. **Check the name and the line.** Does the name help or block the position? Does using the same name on unrelated products blur it (Ries and Trout warn against line extension)? Recommend keep, narrow or rename.
7. **Write the position.** One sentence, then three ways to repeat it consistently (headline, sales opening, product choice), and what to stop saying.

## Output format

```markdown
# Mind position check: [brand] in [category]

## The category, in buyers' words
[term] — evidence: [search terms, reviews, interviews]

## The ladder
| Rung | Brand | Word it owns | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

## Where we stand
[rung, current word, evidence]

## Strategy
**Chosen:** first in the mind / against the leader / new category / reposition a rival — [why]

## The word to own
**[word]** — true because [evidence]; distinct from [leader's word]

## Name and product line
[keep / narrow / rename, and why]

## The position
[One sentence]
- Headline: [line]
- Sales opening: [line]
- Product choice it implies: [choice]
**Stop saying:** [lines]
```

## Example

**Input:** "This example is fictional, including every brand name. We run a regional accounting software brand in Chile, Cuentafácil, sold to small shops. Buyers mostly say 'Librox' or 'Balanzo' when asked for accounting software [our interviews with 20 shop owners: 12 named Balanzo first, 5 Librox, 2 us]. Shop owners in reviews say Balanzo is 'for accountants'. Our strength: shop owners do their own books in 10 minutes a week, and 80% of our customers have no accountant. We also sell Cuentafácil Payroll and Cuentafácil POS."

**Output:**

```markdown
# Mind position check: Cuentafácil in accounting software (Chile)

## The category, in buyers' words
"accounting software" [check: search terms] — evidence: 20 interviews.

## The ladder
| Rung | Brand | Word it owns | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Balanzo | for accountants | named first by 12 of 20; reviews say "for accountants" |
| 2 | Librox | [check] | named first by 5 of 20 |
| 3 | Cuentafácil | none clear | named first by 2 of 20 |

## Where we stand
Third rung, with no word of our own.

## Strategy
**Chosen:** against the leader. Balanzo owns "for accountants"; its opposite, books for owners with no accountant, is open and true for 80% of our customers.

## The word to own
**"without an accountant"** — true because 80% of our customers have none and spend 10 minutes a week; distinct from Balanzo's "for accountants".

## Name and product line
Keep the name: "fácil" supports the position. Payroll and POS under the same name blur it [check: their share of revenue]; lead with books in all messages.

## The position
Accounting software for shop owners who do the books themselves, without an accountant.
- Headline: "Your shop's books in 10 minutes a week, no accountant needed."
- Sales opening: "Do you have an accountant, or do you do it yourself?"
- Product choice it implies: every feature must be usable without accounting training.
**Stop saying:** "complete business platform", "for every business".
```

## Checks before you send

- [ ] The ladder uses buyers' words and cites evidence, or is marked "[check: buyer research]".
- [ ] One strategy is chosen, with the reason.
- [ ] One word is chosen; it is true and distinct from the leader's.
- [ ] The answer says what to stop saying.
- [ ] No share, survey result or ranking is invented.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing several words. A brand that stands for everything owns nothing.
- Attacking the leader's strength head-on instead of the weakness inside it.
- Picking a word that isn't true. The mind rejects a claim that the product contradicts.
- Stretching one name over unrelated products, then wondering why the brand means nothing.
