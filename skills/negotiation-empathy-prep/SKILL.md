# Negotiation prep with tactical empathy

Chris Voss, a former FBI hostage negotiator, wrote Never Split the Difference (2016) with journalist Tahl Raz. Its argument is that people decide with emotion more than logic, so the negotiator's first job is to understand and show understanding of the other side's position, which Voss calls tactical empathy. His tools include mirroring (repeating the last few words to invite more), labeling ("It seems like…") to name an emotion, an accusation audit (saying the worst things the other side may think of you before they do), calibrated questions that start with "how" or "what", asking for a no instead of pushing for a yes, and looking for "black swans", unknown facts that change the deal. This skill prepares one negotiation with those tools and a firm limit.

## When to use

- Before a price, contract, renewal, salary or vendor negotiation.
- When the other side has said no, or gone silent.
- When a deal keeps stalling and nobody knows why.
- Not for replying to a written sales objection: use objection-handling. Not for a hard conversation with your own report: use difficult-conversation-prep.

## What you need

- What you want, the least you'd accept, and your best alternative if there's no deal.
- What you know about the other side: their goals, pressures, deadline, who else decides.
- The history: offers made, objections raised, tone so far.
- If the counterpart's constraints are unknown, mark them "[check]" and turn them into questions. Never assume their budget or deadline.

## Steps

1. **Set your limits first.** Write your goal, your walk-away point and your best alternative to a deal. These don't move during the talk.
2. **Map the other side.** List what they likely want, fear and must report to someone else. Mark guesses "[check]". List the unknowns that could change the deal (the black swans to look for).
3. **Write the accusation audit.** List the 3 to 5 worst things they might think about you or your offer, and phrase each as a label to say early ("You probably think we're asking for too much").
4. **Write labels for their emotions.** Draft 3 labels for what they may feel ("It seems like the timing is what worries you"), to use when you hear it, then stay silent.
5. **Write calibrated questions.** Draft 5 to 7 how and what questions that make them solve the problem with you ("How am I supposed to do that?", "What's making this hard for you?", "How will your team decide?"). Avoid "why", which sounds like blame.
6. **Plan questions that invite a no.** Draft 2 to 3 questions where "no" feels safe for them and moves the deal ("Is it a bad idea to…?", "Have you given up on…?").
7. **Plan the money sequence.** If price is at stake, plan the anchor and the steps of any concession in advance, with odd, specific numbers and something non-monetary to offer. Voss describes a stepped sequence (sometimes called the Ackerman model); use it only with a ceiling you set in step 1.
8. **Plan the close.** Write how you'll check real agreement: a summary they confirm ("That's right"), and how they'll carry it out ("How will we know it's on track?").

## Example

**Input:** "I run a 6-person design studio in Mexico City. A food brand wants a packaging redesign for 12 products. I quoted 380,000 pesos. Their procurement lead, Lucía, replied: 'Our budget is 250,000. Another agency quoted 240,000.' My costs are about 210,000; I won't go below 300,000 because it would delay another client. If this falls through, I have a smaller job worth 120,000 waiting. They want it before their May trade fair."

**Output:**

```markdown
# Negotiation prep: packaging redesign, 12 products

**Date:** [date] · **Counterpart:** Lucía, procurement lead

## My limits
**Goal:** 380,000 pesos, or less scope for less money · **Walk away at:** below 300,000 for the full scope · **Best alternative:** the 120,000 job waiting

## Their side
| What they likely want / fear / answer to | Evidence or [check] |
|---|---|
| Stay in a 250,000 budget | her reply |
| Packaging ready for the May trade fair | the brief |
| Justify the choice to her boss or marketing [check] | procurement usually reports to a budget owner [check] |
**Unknowns to look for:** whether the 240,000 quote covers all 12 products; who in marketing chose us; whether the fair date is fixed.

## Accusation audit (say early)
1. "You probably think we're expensive compared with the other agency."
2. "It may seem like we haven't listened to your budget."

## Labels to use when I hear it
- If she repeats the budget: "It sounds like the budget was set before the scope was clear." — then pause
- If she mentions the fair: "It seems like May is what really matters here." — then pause

## Calibrated questions
1. "What does the other quote include for the 12 products?"
2. "How would you like us to handle the scope if the budget is fixed?"
3. "What happens on your side if the packaging isn't ready for May?"
4. "How does your team decide between the two agencies?"
5. "How am I supposed to deliver all 12 by May at 250,000?"

## Questions that invite a no
1. "Is it a bad idea to start with the 6 products for the fair and do the rest after?"
2. "Would it be unreasonable to fix the price now and agree the schedule after a first review?"

## Offer sequence
| Step | Offer | Non-money item |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 fair products for 205,000 | ready 3 weeks before the fair |
| 2 | all 12 for 342,000 | a second review round |
| 3 | all 12 for 317,500, the last step | 50% of the fee upfront [check: my cash needs] |
Never below 300,000 for all 12.

## Close
**Summary to confirm:** "So what matters is May for the fair products, within a budget you can defend — is that right?" · **Implementation question:** "How will we know at the first review that we're on track?"
```

## Checks before you send

- [ ] The walk-away point and best alternative are set before any tactics.
- [ ] The counterpart's budget, deadline and pressures come from the input or are marked "[check]".
- [ ] Questions start with how or what; none starts with why.
- [ ] The offer sequence never crosses the walk-away point.
- [ ] Nothing in the plan relies on lying about facts, rivals or deadlines.

## Common mistakes

- Splitting the difference to end the discomfort. Voss's title is the warning: a midpoint often leaves both sides worse off.
- Using labels as a script. They work only when they name what you actually heard.
- Pushing for "yes". A forced yes is often a polite stall; a safe "no" tells you more.
- Arguing with numbers before you know their real constraint.
