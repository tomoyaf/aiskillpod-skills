# Business system prototype

Michael E. Gerber founded his E-Myth company in 1977 and published The E-Myth in 1986 and The E-Myth Revisited in 1995. His starting point is a myth: that most small businesses are started by entrepreneurs. Gerber argues most are started by technicians, people good at the work itself (a plumber, a baker, a developer), who assume that knowing the technical work means knowing how to run a business that does it. He calls that the fatal assumption. He describes three roles in every owner: the technician who does the work, the manager who brings order, and the entrepreneur who looks ahead. His answer is to work on the business, not only in it: design it like a franchise prototype, where documented systems let ordinary trained people deliver the same result every time, whether or not the business is ever franchised. This skill shows an owner where their time goes and builds the first pieces of that system.

## When to use

- The business stops when the owner is ill or on holiday.
- The owner works long weeks doing client work and can't find time to plan.
- Before the first hires, to define positions rather than hire for the owner's tasks.
- Not for writing one detailed procedure: use sop-writer for each process this skill names. Not for one-off delegation of a task: use delegation-brief.

## What you need

- What the business does, for whom, and how it makes money.
- The owner's typical week, with hours by activity.
- The people (if any) and what each does.
- What goes wrong when the owner isn't there.
- If hours are missing, ask once for an estimate and mark it "[estimate]". Never invent results.

## Steps

1. **Sort the owner's hours by role.** Label each activity technician (doing the work), manager (organizing, checking, hiring, cash), or entrepreneur (vision, offers, markets). Total the hours per role.
2. **Name the fatal assumption at work.** Say in one sentence how the business depends on the owner's technical skill, with an example from the input.
3. **Write the primary aim and strategic objective.** The owner's own aim (what they want their life to look like) and the business's objective: who it serves, what it promises, the revenue it needs, and the date by which it should run without the owner's daily work. Gerber puts the owner's life first.
4. **Draw the org chart of positions.** List the positions the business needs once grown (for example: delivery, sales, finance, operations), each with its result, even if one person holds several today. Put the owner's name in each position they hold now.
5. **Pick three processes to systemize first.** Choose the ones that take most of the owner's technician hours or cause most trouble when the owner is away. For each, name the result it must produce every time, the trigger, and the checks.
6. **Define "same result every time".** For each process, write the standard a customer would notice (speed, presentation, communication), so someone else can meet it.
7. **Plan the hand-off.** For each process, who will run it, what training they need, and when the owner stops doing it. End with the owner's target hours by role in 6 months.

## Example

**Input:** "I run a 4-person kitchen renovation business in Toronto. My week (about 62 hours): on site doing carpentry 30, quoting and visiting new clients 12, ordering materials 8, bookkeeping 5, answering client calls 7. My two carpenters wait for me to tell them each morning what to do. When I was sick for a week, two jobs stopped. My partner does the books on weekends. I'd like to work 45 hours and stop working Saturdays."

**Output:**

```markdown
# Business system prototype: kitchen renovations

## The owner's week by role
| Activity | Hours | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Carpentry on site | 30 | technician |
| Quoting and client visits | 12 | technician [the selling part is entrepreneur work; mostly technical estimating today] |
| Ordering materials | 8 | manager |
| Bookkeeping | 5 | manager |
| Client calls | 7 | manager |
**Totals:** technician 42 · manager 20 · entrepreneur 0

## The fatal assumption here
The business runs on your carpentry and judgment: the carpenters wait for your instructions each morning, so a week of illness stopped two jobs.

## Aims
**Owner's primary aim:** 45 hours a week, no Saturdays. · **Strategic objective:** [check: who you serve and the promise, the revenue needed], running without your daily site work by [check: date].

## Positions
| Position | Result it owns | Held by today |
|---|---|---|
| Lead carpenter | each job built to standard and on schedule | you |
| Estimator and sales | quotes out within [check: days], jobs won | you |
| Purchasing | materials on site the day before they're needed | you |
| Client communication | every client updated [check: how often] | you |
| Finance | books current, invoices paid | your partner, weekends |

## First three processes
| Process | Result every time | Trigger | Checks | Customer-visible standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Daily job plan | each carpenter knows the day's tasks without asking | job scheduled | plan sent by [check: time] the day before | the client sees work progress daily |
| Materials ordering | everything on site a day early | job plan for next week | order list matched to the plan | no idle days waiting for parts |
| Client update | client knows progress and next step | end of each week | update sent with photos | a weekly update every Friday |

## Hand-off
| Process | New owner | Training | Owner stops by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily job plan | your senior carpenter [check] | 2 weeks writing plans with you | [check: date] |
| Materials ordering | [check: hire or carpenter] | the order checklist | [check] |
| Client update | [check] | a template and examples | [check] |
**In 6 months:** technician [check] · manager [check] · entrepreneur at least [check: hours] — total 45.
```

## Checks before you send

- [ ] Every hour in the input is labelled with a role, and the totals add up.
- [ ] The positions are roles with results, not people.
- [ ] Exactly three processes are chosen, each with a result, trigger, checks and a customer-visible standard.
- [ ] The plan reaches the owner's stated aim, or says what's missing to reach it.
- [ ] Hours and results come from the input or are marked "[estimate]" or "[check]".

## Common mistakes

- Hiring someone to "help" without a position and a result. The owner stays the system.
- Writing every process at once. Start with the three that free the most hours.
- Systems that depend on rare talent. Gerber's prototype is built so ordinary trained people get the same result.
- Forgetting the owner's own aim. The system serves the life the owner wants.
